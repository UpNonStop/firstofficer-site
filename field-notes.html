Field note 01 · What a point is worth

A point is not a point.
Not even close.

The same 50,000 points can be worth about $500 as cash back, or $2,500 to $4,000 toward a business-class trip, est. The balance tells you nothing until you know how it will be spent.

01Learn

The value ladder is hidden

Programs show the simple options first: statement credit, gift cards. The richer ones, transfers to airline and hotel partners, sit behind menus and partner lists.

02Learn

Cents per point is abstract

“1.6 cents a point” means little. “The ad spend paid for a hotel night” means a lot. It is the same math, est., so the desk always says it in dollars.

03Learn

Prices move by the day

Many programs price awards by demand. The same flight can cost very different points on different days, so a fixed rule of thumb breaks.

Statement credit or cash backabout 1¢
Travel portal1¢ to 1.5¢
Economy flight or hotel night1.5¢ to 2¢
Business class5¢ to 8¢
First classup to 12¢

Typical ranges, est. Every estimate on this site counts a point at about 1¢ to 2¢, on purpose. Premium cabins are the upside, never the promise.

Field note 02 · The floor most people settle for

2% cash back feels like winning.
It is the floor.

Cash back is simple and safe, and often the least valuable use of a point you already earned, est.

01Learn

A statement credit is the floor

50,000 points as a statement credit come to about $500. Moved to a travel partner for a trip you wanted anyway, they can be worth more, est.

02Learn

Simple is a design choice

Cash back keeps the transfer partners out of sight. If you never learn that points can move to airline and hotel programs, you keep taking the floor.

03Learn

The gap adds up

A business spending $60,000 a year at 2% gets about $1,200. Routed and redeemed with a plan, the same spend can be worth more in travel, est. Sometimes it is not, and the desk says so.

Field note 03 · Why it is this hard

The rules are complex.
That is not an accident.

Every layer of complexity moves a little value from your account to the program’s.

01Learn

Charts gave way to moving prices

Award charts once told you what an award cost. Many programs now price by demand, with no published chart to plan against.

02Learn

Bonus categories depend on codes

A bonus applies only when the merchant’s category code matches. A grocery pickup or a warehouse club may not code as a grocery store, and few people are told.

03Learn

Transfer ratios are not standard

Many bank points move 1:1 to airline partners. Some move at less, and some hotel points move at about 3:1. Each ratio can change.

Field note 04 · When it has to happen

The right answer at the wrong time
is the wrong answer.

Transfer bonuses, award releases and credit resets all run on clocks nobody publishes in one place.

01Learn

Transfer windows open and close

Programs sometimes add bonus points to transfers to one partner for a short window. Move the same points a day later and they go at the usual ratio.

02Learn

Award space is released on schedules

Many airlines release award space about 11 months ahead, and the best cabins tend to go first, est.

03Learn

Credits reset on different calendars

Calendar year, card year, quarterly, monthly. One card can carry four reset dates.

Field note 05 · What quietly disappears

Your rewards have a shelf life
nobody tells you about.

Points expire, credits reset and certificates lapse. None of it announces itself, and most of it is preventable.

01Learn

Miles expire on different clocks

Some programs expire miles after 18 months without activity, some after 24 and some never. Each one is a trap if nobody is watching.

02Learn

Credits reset on staggered dates

One premium card can carry an airline credit, a hotel credit, a monthly ride credit and a twice-yearly retail credit, each on its own schedule.

03Learn

Free night certificates lapse

A hotel card’s free night is usually valid for about 12 months. It does not roll over, and you paid for it in the annual fee.

Field note 06 · Who the programs serve

Loyalty programs are not loyal
to you.

You are asked to be loyal to a program that can change its terms whenever it likes.

01Learn

You earn blind

Thirty swipes a month, and few owners know whether each one earned 1x, 3x or 4x. The same pump can code as fuel or as a convenience store.

02Learn

You redeem blind

A hotel night for 40,000 points can be a great or a poor use of them, depending on the date. The program does not say which.

03Learn

Prices go up

Programs revalue awards. A trip that costs 60,000 miles this year can cost more next year.

Field note 07 · Who the advice serves

When the advice is paid for,
who is it for?

The facts in most card content are accurate. The order they appear in is often paid for.

01Learn

Rankings can follow referral fees

Many card sites earn a fee when you sign up through them. Cards that pay more tend to get more space and a higher spot.

02Learn

Comparison tables can be placements

A card that pays no referral fee may not appear in the table at all.

03Learn

Right facts, bought framing

You get correct information, arranged toward the conclusion that pays the publisher most.

Field note 08 · What actually works

A dashboard waits for you.
A desk reaches out.

Information you have to remember to go and look at is information you will not use.

01Learn

Dashboards go unchecked

Most tracking apps get opened a lot in week one and rarely after. An expiring balance does not wait for you to log in.

02Learn

Data without context is noise

“85,000 points.” Is it a lot? Enough for the trip? About to expire? A number alone answers none of it.

03Learn

Reactive is too late

The useful message arrives before the deadline: these miles expire in 30 days, here is the fix, about five minutes.
